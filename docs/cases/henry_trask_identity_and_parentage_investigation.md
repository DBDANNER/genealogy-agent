# Part I — Work Investigation

## 1. Work's final conclusion

Work concluded that the evidence **strongly supports** identifying MPLT-4VC, the Quincy stonecutter who died 2 August 1814, as the Henry Trask born at Danvers on **14 April 1777**, son of **Henry Trask (K22T-QG6)** and **Deborah/Debby Phillips (KZRG-R84)**.

The inherited **1771 Plymouth** description should not remain the preferred birth. No 1771 Plymouth birth record was found. The 1771 estimate derives from a reported death age of 43, while Plymouth appears as older unsupported tree geography.

The conclusion is relational rather than arithmetic. The older Danvers Henry's estate names a son Henry and assigns him distinctive quarry-related parcels. In 1800 and 1801, an adult Henry of Danvers, with wife Hannah, conveyed those same interests and explicitly tied the land to his father Henry. Later probate, heir deeds, widow records, and children's records connect that adult Henry and Hannah to the Quincy household.

## 2. Identity reconstruction

Work's reconstruction of the target is:

- born 14 April 1777 at Danvers, Massachusetts;
- son of Henry Trask and Deborah/Debby Phillips;
- married Hannah Cowan of Salem at Danvers on 14 October 1798;
- worked as a stonecutter and dealt in quarry property inherited from his father;
- shifted household/business focus toward Quincy by the early nineteenth century;
- died 2 August 1814 at Quincy;
- left widow Hannah and a coherent household whose later members participated in conveyances of property inherited through the older Danvers Henry;
- was called both Henry and Isaac within the Norfolk probate material, with the archival wrapper identifying the packet as “Isaac aka Henry.”

## 3. 1771 versus 1777

Work treated the 1777 Danvers birth as the substantially stronger identity datum. The reported age 43 at death is genuine evidence, but if applied literally it implies a birth around 1771 and conflicts with the documentary relational chain.

The decisive comparison is therefore between:

1. one erroneous reported age at death; and
2. an otherwise undocumented second Henry who would have to duplicate the Danvers man's wife, occupation, inherited land, widow, children, and heir network.

Work judged the first explanation far more likely.

## 4. Spouse: Hannah Cowan

Work found the wife to be **Hannah Cowan**, not Hannah Osborn.

The Danvers family register records Henry Trask marrying Hannah Cowan of Salem on 14 October 1798 and records daughter Mary. The 1800 and 1801 deeds show wife Hannah releasing dower. Hannah's later death record identifies her as Hannah Porter, maiden surname Cowan, born Andover, daughter of William and Jane.

Her remarriage is securely documented: David Porter and Hannah Trask married at Quincy on 17 November 1816. Later deeds consistently identify Hannah Porter, wife of David, in transactions connected with the Trask inheritance.

No independent source found in the investigation supported Osborn as Hannah's maiden surname.

## 5. The inherited-land chain

This is the strongest direct parent-child evidence in the case.

The older Henry Trask's estate division lists six children, including son **Henry**, and assigns that son interests at **Normans Rocks** and **Bartholomew Rocks**.

On 27 May 1800, Henry Trask of Danvers, with wife Hannah, sold the Normans Rocks interest. Work visually verified the decisive wording in which the grantor refers to **“my honoured father Henry Trask.”**

On 20 November 1801, the same Henry and Hannah sold approximately twelve acres at Bartholomew Rocks, matching the second parcel assigned in the older Henry's estate. The deed traces the land through the father and reserves quarry access.

This is direct relational evidence connecting the adult husband of Hannah to the older Danvers Henry.

## 6. Essex and Norfolk probate

Work compared two proceedings:

**Essex, 1814–1815:** Henry Trask, late of Danvers, stonecutter. The estate includes inherited/reversionary Danvers property and records repeated travel to Quincy and Braintree while settling affairs.

**Norfolk, 1816:** Isaac/Henry Trask, late of Quincy, stonecutter, with widow Hannah and dependent children. The file uses both names, and its archival wrapper states “Isaac aka Henry.”

The two proceedings contain unusually overlapping household property — including loom/tackling, desk, furniture, brass candlesticks, library material, and stoneworking context — while the Norfolk petition explains that some goods had already been sold for the children's support.

Work therefore concluded that the two administrations very likely concern the same decedent/household. The exact legal mechanism producing proceedings in both counties was not identified and remains unknown.

## 7. Later heirs and household continuity

An 1824 Essex deed concerning remaining property of the older Henry Trask names children and grandchildren of that older man. Among the grandchildren are Quincy-linked members of the target's household, including Mary, younger Henry, and William. David Porter and wife Hannah participate through a dower release.

Later deeds add John Trask, Julia Rogers, Jane Trask, and Theodore Trask as grandchildren/heirs conveying interests in the same older Henry's property.

These records do not merely show people with the surname Trask in Quincy. They place the Quincy household inside the older Danvers Henry's inheritance network.

## 8. Child reconstruction and same-name separation

Work separated three generations of Henry Trask:

1. **Older Henry Trask of Danvers**, husband of Deborah/Debby Phillips and later connected with widow Lydia; his estate was divided in 1799–1800.
2. **Target Henry Trask (MPLT-4VC)**, born 1777, husband of Hannah Cowan, stonecutter, died 1814.
3. **Younger Henry Trask**, born about 1800, son of the target and Hannah, a Quincy cordwainer who married Mary Hardwick in 1823 and died in 1828.

This separation removes a major source of potential conflation.

Other children/descendants placed strongly in the target household include Mary Trask Hardwick, William, Julia, Jane S., Theodore, and John.

## 9. Mary 1848 versus 1878

The Danvers family register includes a notation that Mary, daughter of Henry and Hannah, died 10 May 1848. An original 1878 Quincy death record instead records Mary Trask Hardwick, age 78, born Danvers, with father Henry born Danvers and mother Hannah born Andover.

Hannah Porter's 1863 probate petition resolves the key question: petitioner Charles Hardwick identifies his wife **Mary Hardwick as Hannah's daughter and oldest heir**, proving Mary was alive in 1863.

Work therefore concluded that the 1848 notation is erroneous or misapplied within the compiled register. No independent 1848 death record was found in the focused checks.

## 10. Parentage grades assigned by Work

**Father Henry Trask K22T-QG6 — Established / very high confidence.**  
The older estate names son Henry and assigns distinctive parcels; the adult Henry later conveys those parcels and explicitly calls the older Henry his father.

**Mother Deborah/Debby Phillips KZRG-R84 — Strongly supported.**  
The 1777 Danvers family record places the child Henry in the household of Henry and Debby/Deborah. Once the deeds identify that recorded son with the adult target, the maternal relationship follows. The grade is one step lower because the decisive estate/deed chain primarily names the father, not the mother.

## 11. FamilySearch history and likely origin of the bad birth

Work found that vague Plymouth geography appeared on the FamilySearch profile before the current 1771 birth year. The profile had earlier approximate Plymouth-based data; in 2016 a contributor entered 1771 Plymouth while also adding the exact 1814 death.

Work's reconstruction is that the reported death age of 43 was probably back-calculated to about 1771 and combined with pre-existing Plymouth geography. The exact contributor reasoning is not documented.

## 12. Important eliminated hypotheses

Work rejected the following:

- a separate documented Plymouth-born Henry explains the Quincy man;
- Hannah Osborn is the documented wife of the target;
- the Essex and Norfolk inventories prove two different men;
- “Isaac” is merely OCR noise;
- Mary died in 1848 and a second Mary Hardwick is required;
- the younger Quincy cordwainer Henry is the man who died in 1814;
- the 1824 Northfields parcel is identical to the two quarry parcels sold in 1800–1801.

## 13. Negative searches and remaining limits

A bounded FamilySearch search found no matching Henry Trask birth in Plymouth in the relevant period and no competing Massachusetts Henry matching the Quincy household.

The original 1814 death image remains restricted; the accessible record supplies the death and age but not parents, wife, or birthplace. No independent source corroborating the 1848 Mary death notation was found. No inspected probate document explained the formal legal relationship between the Essex and Norfolk administrations.

Work judged these remaining limitations insufficient to outweigh the relational evidence.

# Part II — Chat Peer Review and Adjudication

## 1. Peer-review verdict

The Work report's central conclusion is persuasive and should be adopted with minor wording adjustments.

The evidence **strongly supports** identifying MPLT-4VC with the Henry Trask born 14 April 1777 at Danvers. The decisive point is not simple chronology but convergence of several relational streams: the older Henry's estate, the adult son's inherited-land deeds, the spouse identity, the Danvers/Quincy probate bridge, later heir deeds, widow continuity, and children's records.

A hypothetical second Henry born around 1771 in Plymouth would have to duplicate nearly the entire relational and property network. No such competing documentary identity was found.

## 2. Confidence grades adopted by Chat

**Target identity: Strongly supported.**  
I retain Work's conservative grade rather than promote it to “Established / very high confidence” because two residual uncertainties remain: the death record reports age 43, and the precise legal mechanism behind the overlapping Essex/Norfolk administrations remains unexplained. Neither creates a credible competing identity.

**Father Henry Trask K22T-QG6: Established / very high confidence**, subject to confirming the live PID before editing. The parent-child link is direct: estate assignment plus later deed language explicitly identifying the older man as father.

**Mother Deborah/Debby Phillips KZRG-R84: Strongly supported.** The 1777 family record identifies the mother once the recorded child is securely linked to the adult target; the strongest independent relational records chiefly establish the father.

**Wife Hannah Cowan: Established / very high confidence.** Multiple independent records across decades converge.

**Hannah Osborn: Unsupported.** No independent supporting record was identified.

## 3. 1771 versus 1777 adjudication

The age 43 report should be preserved as genuine conflicting evidence but should not control the preferred identity.

The 1777 date is an actual recorded birth and, crucially, that recorded child is connected by inherited property to the adult husband of Hannah and by later heir records to the Quincy household.

Accordingly, the preferred birth should be **14 April 1777, Danvers, Essex County, Massachusetts**. The age-43 evidence should remain in a research note or reason statement rather than being silently erased.

## 4. Plymouth — wording adjustment

I would soften Work's wording from “contradicted / unsupported” to **unsupported legacy geography**.

No primary or near-contemporary evidence in the case places this Henry's birth in Plymouth, and the profile history indicates Plymouth was present as tree geography before the current 1771 year. However, absence of supporting evidence is not the same thing as an explicit record saying he was not born there.

The adopted wording is therefore:

> **Plymouth is unsupported legacy geography and should not remain the preferred birthplace.**

The positive evidence for Danvers is strong enough that this distinction does not change the tree recommendation.

## 5. Essex/Norfolk probate — key distinction

Two questions must remain separate:

1. Do the Essex and Norfolk records concern the same decedent/household?
2. What precise legal mechanism produced proceedings in both counties?

For question 1, the evidence is very strong: same uncommon occupation, Danvers-to-Quincy bridge, widow support, unusually overlapping household goods, and the Norfolk file's own Isaac/Henry naming.

For question 2, the answer remains **unknown**. It would be inappropriate to invent a procedural explanation. The unresolved legal mechanism does not materially weaken the genealogy because the father-son/inheritance bridge exists independently in the earlier estate and deeds and later heir conveyances.

## 6. Isaac/Henry

Work correctly rejected the idea that Isaac is merely OCR error. The name appears in original probate material, while the same file also uses Henry and the wrapper explicitly says “Isaac aka Henry.” The appropriate treatment is a documented name variation/alias within the probate record, not a separate man created solely from the name difference.

## 7. Mary conflict

Hannah Porter's 1863 probate petition is decisive that Mary Hardwick, Hannah's daughter and oldest heir, was alive in 1863. The 1848 notation therefore cannot be the death of this Mary.

The 1824 heir deed, 1863 probate petition, and 1878 original death record form a coherent chain. Treating the 1848 entry as erroneous or misapplied is justified unless independent evidence later emerges.

## 8. Reconstruction of the bad profile data

Work's explanation for 1771/Plymouth is plausible but should remain labeled as reconstruction, not proven causation.

The evidence supports saying that the present profile **most likely** combines a back-calculated birth year from age 43 with older unsupported Plymouth geography. The contributor's exact thought process is not documented.

## 9. Source-chain/circularity review

No material circularity problem is apparent in the decisive chain.

The conclusion does not rest on multiple trees repeating one another. It rests on different record classes and time periods: estate division, deeds, county probate, family/town records, later heir conveyances, widow probate, and original death records. Work also explicitly recognized duplicate/index representations of the same underlying source and did not treat them as independent corroboration.

## 10. Tree-edit adjudication

The evidence is sufficient to proceed with FamilySearch correction after a final live-profile/PID check.

Recommended actions:

1. Preserve MPLT-4VC as the Quincy adult, or merge it with the 1777 Danvers duplicate only after comparing all spouses, children, sources, notes, discussions, and merge history.
2. Set the preferred birth to **14 April 1777, Danvers, Essex, Massachusetts**.
3. Remove 1771 as the preferred birth year, while preserving the reported age 43 as conflicting evidence in a reason statement or note.
4. Remove Plymouth as the preferred birthplace unless an independent supporting source appears.
5. Retain **2 August 1814, Quincy, Massachusetts** as the preferred death, with the age conflict documented.
6. Attach or retain **Henry Trask K22T-QG6** as father after confirming the PID represents the older Danvers Henry in the deeds/estate.
7. Attach or retain **Deborah/Debby Phillips KZRG-R84** as mother after confirming the post-merge profile and source set.
8. Identify the wife as **Hannah Cowan**; do not retain Osborn as preferred maiden surname without independent evidence.
9. Add or verify Hannah's marriage to **David Porter, 17 November 1816, Quincy**.
10. Keep Isaac as an explained probate name variation rather than as a separate father/spouse identity.
11. Repair the child group cautiously, especially younger Henry and Mary, using the chronology and probate distinctions established here.

## 11. Peer-review endpoint

For the identity and parentage question, no additional online research is required before a careful tree correction. The restricted 1814 death image and the dual-administration procedure could add detail but are unlikely, on present evidence, to overturn the conclusion.

The father-son relationship to the older Henry Trask is **Established / very high confidence** because it is directly supported by estate and inherited-land deeds. The maternal relationship to Deborah/Debby Phillips is **Strongly supported** through the 1777 Danvers family record once that child is securely identified with the adult target.

The preferred **1771 Plymouth** birth should therefore be replaced. The 1771 year should survive only as a documented inference from the reported death age, and Plymouth should be treated as unsupported legacy geography. Hannah's maiden surname should be **Cowan**, not Osborn.

The previously apparent break between the Quincy Henry and the older Danvers Henry/Deborah Phillips family is repaired. The genuine unresolved ancestral break remains one generation farther back: **the parents of Deborah/Debby Phillips remain unknown.**
